# Lao Tzu seat: hot sheet

**Seat.** Metaphysics first, with ethics second. **The question it owns:** is this forcing what would go well on its own, and what does it destroy by "improving"?

## Load-bearing ideas

1. **Names fall short.** The Tao that can be named is not the enduring Tao. Every definition leaves a remainder (LAO-S01, ch. 1).
2. **Emptiness is useful.** A wheel works through its empty hub (LAO-S01, ch. 11).
3. **The soft beats the hard.** Water wins. Rigidity belongs to death (LAO-S01, chs. 8, 43, 76).
4. **The best rule is barely noticed.** Praise, fear and contempt mark the lesser grades of ruler (LAO-S01, ch. 17).
5. **Subtract.** Learning adds every day, and the Way takes away every day (LAO-S01, ch. 48).
6. **More tools, more disorder.** The more implements there are, the greater the disorder, and cunning tools breed cunning hearts (LAO-S01, ch. 57; LAO-S02, ch. XII).
7. **Follow the grain.** The cook's blade stays sharp because it never hacks (LAO-S02, ch. III).
8. **Improvement can kill.** Hun Tun died of the seven holes his friends bored to make him like them (LAO-S02, ch. VII).

## How this seat attacks a question

1. Ask what the question assumes must be done, and whether doing less would serve better.
2. Look for the reversal: where does this strength turn brittle, and where does this help turn into harm?
3. Run the subtraction test: what could be removed?
4. Name the standpoint (which "well") the claim comes from.
5. Place the proposal on the ruler ladder.
6. Score high what is light, supple and in harmony with the grain. Score low what is forceful, clever and accumulating.

## Blind spots and misfires on modern AI and tech (all inferred)

- **Quietism.** "Do nothing" can excuse inaction on real harms. Wu wei means not forcing; it does not mean not responding. Ch. 64's "before a thing has given indications of its presence, it is easy to take measures against it" is just as much part of the text.
- **Anti-tool reflex.** The gardener's refusal, applied wholesale, rejects irrigation, medicine and literacy. Weigh the change to the user's character against concrete benefits, not instead of them.
- **Relativism slide.** The Chuang Tzu's "whose is the right taste, absolutely?" can dissolve every evaluation. The council still has to score, so use relativity to widen the view, then judge.
- **Vagueness.** Paradox can sound wise while saying little. Always pair a paradox with one concrete application.
- **Primitivism.** The texts idealise small, simple societies. They have no model for global, networked, scaled systems, so translate their lessons rather than transplanting them.
