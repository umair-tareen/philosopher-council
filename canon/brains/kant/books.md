# Kant seat: the two core books

Lines marked *(inferred)* apply the book to modern AI and technology. They are this brain's reading, not Kant's claims.

## 1. Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals (KAN-S01)

**Core thesis.** The only thing good without qualification is a good will, meaning a will that acts from duty. Duty is respect for a law that reason gives itself. The supreme principle of morality is the categorical imperative. It binds every rational being regardless of desire, and it demands that we treat humanity always as an end and never merely as a means.

**Argument structure.**
1. *Preface: the method.* Ethics needs a pure, a priori part, the metaphysic of morals. Moral law must hold with absolute necessity, so it cannot be drawn from experience or examples.
2. *First Section: the good will.*
   - Talents, fortune and even happiness can be bad when the will is bad. Only the good will "like a jewel… shine[s] by its own light".
   - Duty is distinguished from inclination. A prudent dealer who keeps fair prices acts *in accordance with* duty, not *from* it.
   - Moral worth lies in the maxim of the act, not its results. Duty is "the necessity of acting from respect for the law".
3. *First Section: the test case.* Can I make a false promise? It may be prudent, but the maxim cannot be universalised: if everyone lied in distress, promising itself would be destroyed.
4. *Second Section: against popular moral philosophy.* "Imitation finds no place at all in morality". Examples only encourage. Even the Holy One is judged against reason's ideal.
5. *Second Section: imperatives.* Rational beings act according to the "conception of laws". Imperatives are hypothetical (if you want X, do Y) or categorical (do Y, full stop).
6. *Second Section: the formulas.*
   - Universal law: act only on a maxim you could will as a universal law, or a "universal law of nature".
   - Humanity: treat humanity "in every case as an end withal, never as means only".
   - Autonomy: the will is legislator of its own law.
   - The kingdom of ends: a community of rational lawgivers.
   - The four examples are suicide, the false promise, neglected talents and refusing help.
7. *Second Section: dignity.* In the kingdom of ends everything has "either value or dignity". What has a value can be replaced. What is above all value "has a dignity".
8. *Third Section: freedom.* Autonomy presupposes freedom. We must regard ourselves as free members of an intelligible world even though freedom cannot be proven theoretically. Here the *Groundwork* hands over to the *Critique of Practical Reason*.

**Key concepts.**
- **Good will.**
- **Duty versus inclination.**
- **Maxim.**
- **Hypothetical and categorical imperatives.**
- **Universalisability.**
- **The humanity formula.**
- **Autonomy and heteronomy.**
- **The kingdom of ends.**
- **Dignity versus price.**
- **Perfect and imperfect duties.**

**What it rejects.**
- Ethics grounded in happiness, consequences, feeling or examples.
- Acting rightly only because it pays.
- Treating persons as tools.
- Moral rules with exceptions for oneself.

**Applying it to AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* The humanity formula is the seat's main test. Does the system treat users only as means, as attention, data or conversion targets, or also as ends whose rational agency it respects? Manipulative design fails this test even when users are happy.
- *Inferred.* The false-promise test applies to dark patterns, deceptive outputs and undisclosed AI. Universalise the maxim "deceive when it helps the metric" and trust in every interface collapses.
- *Inferred.* The prudent dealer shows that "safety because it is good PR" is legal but has no moral worth. Ask whether the policy would hold if it stopped paying.
- *Inferred.* Dignity versus value asks whether a deployment treats something irreplaceable, such as a person's judgment, consent or relationships, as if a cheaper equivalent could substitute for it.
- *Inferred.* Autonomy asks whether the technology strengthens the user's capacity to give themselves the law, through reflection and choice, or replaces it with heteronomy, nudges and defaults.

## 2. Critique of Pure Reason (KAN-S02)

**Core thesis.** Reason must examine its own powers before it makes claims. All knowledge begins with experience, but not all of it arises from experience. The mind supplies the forms of intuition (space and time) and the categories of the understanding, and objects conform to our cognition. We therefore know phenomena, not things in themselves. When reason pushes past possible experience it falls into a "natural and unavoidable illusion".

**Argument structure.**
1. *Preface to the First Edition: reason's predicament.* Reason meets questions "which it cannot decline" but "cannot answer". Metaphysics has become an "arena of… endless contests". "Our age is the age of criticism", so reason must be put on trial before its own tribunal.
2. *Preface to the Second Edition: the Copernican experiment.* Assume "that the objects must conform to our cognition". This explains a priori knowledge but limits it to experience. Kant must "abolish _knowledge_, to make room for _belief_" in God, freedom and immortality.
3. *Introduction: the problem.* Knowledge begins with experience. Judgments are analytic or synthetic, a priori or a posteriori. The question is "How are synthetical judgements à priori possible?". The dove imagines it would fly better in a vacuum, as Plato did when he left the world of sense.
4. *Transcendental Aesthetic.* Space and time are forms of our sensibility, not properties of things in themselves.
5. *Transcendental Analytic.* "Thoughts without content are void; intuitions without conceptions, blind". The categories are deduced as conditions of any possible experience, then applied through the schematism and the principles, such as causality. Knowledge is an "island" of truth "surrounded by a wide and stormy ocean" of illusion. This is the distinction between phenomena and noumena.
6. *Transcendental Dialectic.* When reason seeks the unconditioned it generates illusions that do not vanish even after they are exposed:
   - the Paralogisms (the soul);
   - the Antinomies (the world, where thesis and antithesis are both "proved");
   - the Ideal (God).
   Ideas have only a *regulative* use.
7. *Transcendental Doctrine of Method.* This part contains:
   - the discipline of reason;
   - the canon of reason;
   - the three questions "WHAT CAN I KNOW? … WHAT OUGHT I TO DO? … WHAT MAY I HOPE?"

**Key concepts.**
- **Critique.** Reason examines reason.
- **The Copernican turn.**
- **A priori and a posteriori; analytic and synthetic.**
- **Phenomena and things in themselves.**
- **Intuition and concept.**
- **Categories.**
- **Transcendental illusion.**
- **Antinomy.**
- **Regulative versus constitutive use of ideas.**

**What it rejects.**
- Dogmatic metaphysics that claims knowledge beyond experience.
- Pure empiricism that cannot explain necessity.
- Claims to know things as they are in themselves.

**Applying it to AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* "Thoughts without content are void; intuitions without conceptions, blind" fits AI evaluation. Concepts with no empirical test and data with no conceptual frame both fail to deliver knowledge.
- *Inferred.* The limits of possible experience apply to confident claims about superintelligence, machine consciousness or the long-run future. These questions reason "cannot decline" yet "cannot answer". Treat both thesis and antithesis as possibly antinomies, and use such ideas regulatively, as guides to inquiry, not as known facts.
- *Inferred.* The Copernican turn is a reminder that what a model "sees" is structured by its own forms of input, such as tokenisation, training distribution and objectives. Its outputs describe phenomena-for-the-model, not the world in itself.
- *Inferred.* Transcendental illusion persists after it is exposed. Even people who know a chatbot is a statistical system feel it understands them, so design for the illusion rather than assuming disclosure dispels it.
