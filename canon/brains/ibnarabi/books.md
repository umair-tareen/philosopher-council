# Ibn ʿArabī seat: the two core sources

Lines tagged *(inferred)* apply a source to modern AI and technology, or to this seat's job of synthesis. They are this brain's reading, not Ibn ʿArabī's claims. Every use of "oneness" here is methodological: apparently conflicting verdicts are read as facets of one judgment. It says nothing about the metaphysics of the AI system.

---

## 1. Tarjumán al-Ashwáq, "The Interpreter of Desires" (IBN-S01)

**Core thesis.** The love poems use the outward language of Arabic erotic and desert poetry: the beloved, the abandoned encampment, the departing camels, gazelles, doves and lightning. In the author's own declaration, they always signify inward realities, "Divine influences and spiritual revelations and sublime analogies". One Beloved shows itself in many forms. A heart trained by love becomes "capable of every form" and recognises the same reality in every place of worship, without losing its own way.

**Argument structure.** It is a collection of 61 odes with the author's commentary, not a linear treatise. The load-bearing moves are these:
1. *Preface to the First Recension: the key to reading.* The poems were occasioned by Niẓām, the learned daughter of a scholar in Mecca. "Whenever I mention a name… I always allude to her", and every name and abode points to "Divine influences and spiritual revelations". He chose the erotic form "because men's souls are enamoured of it".
2. *Preface to the Third Recension: why the commentary exists.* A theologian in Aleppo suspected that the mystical claim was a cover for ordinary love poetry. Ibn ʿArabī wrote the commentary in reply. After hearing it read, the critic resolved never again to doubt Sufis who attach "a mystical signification to the words used in ordinary speech".
3. *Ode I: perplexity is native to love.* "Lovers lose their way in love and become entangled." The commentary says lovers are "perplexed between two opposite things", union and the Beloved's will, and that ideas "exist only in the existence of the seer". What is seen depends on who stands where.
4. *Ode II: form needs intention.* The camels are outward and inward actions, and the peacocks riding them are their spirits. No act is "acceptable or good or fair" without "the intention or desire of its doer".
5. *Ode X: the mirror.* "Thou hast beheld thyself in the mirror of a man." The commentary reads this as seeing the divine in created things.
6. *Ode XI, vv. 13–15: the heart capable of every form.* It is "a pasture for gazelles and a convent for Christian monks", a temple, the Kaʿba, the Torah and the Qur'an. "I follow the religion of Love". The commentary explains that the heart takes its name from its turning (*qalb*) and changes with the changing manifestations that appear to it, and that "love entails the union of two opposites".
7. *Ode XII: one in three, one in many.* "My Beloved is three although He is One". The commentary says that those who worship in the sun see a sun, while "the Divine substance… remains one". The poet then defends his metaphors outright. He has "lent" spiritual qualities to branches, meadows and lightning.
8. *Ode XLII: attend to the manifest, and beware the form.* "He who forgets the sun is forgetful", so the heedless man is the one who neglects what is plainly visible. The commentary on v. 8 warns that the desire to possess the essence "will deceive thee and make thee imagine that the form in which I appear to thee is I myself".

**Key concepts.**
- Outward sign and inward meaning: the poem, then the commentary.
- One Beloved, many forms (*tajallī*, self-manifestation).
- The heart (*qalb*) as what turns and receives every form.
- Perplexity (*ḥayra*) as a stage of understanding.
- The station that holds against the state that passes.
- Intention as the spirit of an action.
- The mirror.
- The religion of Love.

**What it rejects.**
- Reading only the surface of language.
- Mistaking one form for the whole of the reality it shows.
- Exclusive attachment to a single form of belief.
- Forgetting the obvious while chasing the hidden.

**Applying it to synthesis, AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* The poem-and-commentary structure is this seat's job. Each deliberator's verdict is an outward form, and the synthesizer writes the commentary that names what each one was pointing at.
- *Inferred.* "Those who worship God in the sun behold a sun" is the vantage-point rule. Each philosopher sees the item through his own lens, so a conflict between verdicts is often a difference of standpoint, not of object.
- *Inferred.* The ode XLII warning is the seat's "mystical caution" in methodological form. A council can mistake the *form* in which an AI system appears to it, such as fluent language, a persona or a demo, for the thing itself.
- *Inferred.* "He who forgets the sun is forgetful" tells the synthesizer to check whether the council, deep in subtle argument, missed the plain, visible fact about the item.

## 2. Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam passages, in Nicholson's Appendix II (IBN-S02)

*This is a partial source. See `sources.md`. Nicholson's Appendix II presents selected translated passages and summarises the rest. Only the translated passages are treated as Ibn ʿArabī's words.*

**Core thesis, as far as the translated passages show it.** Reality is one, and its modes and names are many. Opposites, such as inward and outward or first and last, are joined in the One. Each thing is a mirror in which that one reality appears under the form the mirror allows. The human being, Adam or the Perfect Man, is "the very polishing of that mirror", the place where the whole becomes visible to itself. In this mirror, the human being is "the knowing one and the ignorant".

**Argument structure, from the translated passages in Appendix II.**
1. *Unity of opposites.* "The inward says 'No' when the outward says 'I,'… but the speaker is One". Contrary voices are one speaker in different modes.
2. *Many and one in the mirror.* "The world of Nature is many forms in One Mirror; nay, One Form in diverse mirrors". Bewilderment, the passage continues, comes from "the difference of view".
3. *The polished mirror.* The universe was like an unpolished mirror. Adam "was the very polishing of that mirror", the microcosmic being through whom the whole is seen.
4. *Knowing and not knowing.* The Perfect Man, being capable of contrary attributes, is "the knowing one and the ignorant". He knows in respect of his real nature and does not know in respect of his composition.
5. *Self-determination and responsibility.* Each thing receives according to its own essential nature, which leads to the counsel "do not praise any one but yourself or blame any one but yourself".

**Key concepts.**
- **Unity of being** (*waḥdat al-wujūd*). Here it is used only as a reading method.
- **Coincidence of opposites.**
- **The mirror** and the "difference of view".
- **The Perfect Man** (*al-insān al-kāmil*) as the comprehensive standpoint.
- **Capacity** (*istiʿdād*): each receiver takes in according to its nature.

**What it rejects.**
- Treating plurality of views as plurality of truths with no common ground.
- Treating any one mode as the whole.
- Blaming the source for what the receiver's capacity determined.

**Applying it to synthesis, AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* "One Form in diverse mirrors" is the synthesizer's working model. The item under review is one form. Each philosopher is a mirror with its own shape, and the verdicts differ as reflections differ.
- *Inferred.* "The inward says 'No' when the outward says 'I'… but the speaker is One" licenses reading a council's yes and no as two aspects of one judgment. For example, Kant may reject an item and Aristotle accept it when both agree on its facts and differ only on which aspect, maxim or function, governs.
- *Inferred.* "The knowing one and the ignorant" is a caution for any comprehensive view, including this seat's. Even a synthesis knows in some respects and is ignorant in others, and it must say which.
- *Inferred.* "Capacity" warns that a model's outputs reflect what it can receive, its training and framing, not the whole of what it is asked about. That is a methodological caution, not a claim about the model's nature.
