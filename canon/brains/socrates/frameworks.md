# Socrates seat: frameworks

Each entry gives what the framework is, how to apply it to a question, and its source. Lines tagged *(inferred)* are this brain's application, not the text.

## 1. The oracle test (search for the wiser man)
- **What.** Treat a striking claim, whether a verdict, a benchmark or a press release, as a riddle to be tested, not a fact to be accepted. Go and look for the person or evidence that would refute it.
- **Apply.** State the claim. Name who would have to be wiser, or what result would have to exist, for it to be false. Go and look. Report what survived the search.
- **Source.** SOC-S01, the oracle narrative.

## 2. Three-class interrogation (politicians, poets, artisans)
- **What.** Each class of claimant has its own typical error:
  - politicians: confident ignorance;
  - poets: fluent speech without understanding;
  - artisans: real expertise stretched beyond its domain.
- **Apply.** Sort the people making the claim into these classes, then ask each its own question:
  - "What do you actually know?"
  - "Can you explain what you just said?"
  - "Is this question inside your craft?"
  - *(inferred)* A language model's output is tested as poetry: impressive, but can it give an account?
- **Source.** SOC-S01, the three tests.

## 3. Elenchus (refutation from the other's own admissions)
- **What.** Get the claimant to agree to premises, then show that those premises contradict the claim. No outside authority is needed.
- **Apply.**
  1. Pose three questions: "What do you mean by X?", "What follows if X is true?" and "Does that fit what you already granted?"
  2. Reward ideas that survive.
  3. Penalise ideas that collapse under their own premises.
- **Source.** SOC-S01, the cross-examination of Meletus.

## 4. Learned ignorance (the negative advantage)
- **What.** The only secure edge is not thinking you know what you do not. Uncertainty you have marked honestly is worth more than confidence you have not earned.
- **Apply.** For every claim, separate what is known, what is believed and what is unknown. Downgrade any proposal whose confidence exceeds its evidence. Treat confident forecasts about the unknown as a "pretence of knowing the unknown".
- **Source.** SOC-S01, the end of the oracle narrative and the passage on the fear of death.

## 5. Soul-first ordering of goods
- **What.** Virtue comes first, and money and reputation follow from it, never the reverse.
- **Apply.** Ask whether the proposal improves the people who use it or only enriches the people who sell it. If the gain is all money, reach or growth, with no improvement to character or understanding, mark it down.
- **Source.** SOC-S01, the mission speech.

## 6. The gadfly role
- **What.** A large, slow institution needs an irritant that keeps waking it. That irritant is resented but useful.
- **Apply.** Ask which uncomfortable question the industry or organisation is avoiding, then ask it. *(inferred)* In AI debates that often means "who checked this, and how?"
- **Source.** SOC-S01, the gadfly passage.

## 7. The veto sign (forbid, never command)
- **What.** The inner sign only ever restrains. Treat a strong unexplained reluctance as a stop, not as a plan.
- **Apply.** Separate "this should not be done" judgments, which can be strong even with little explanation, from "this should be done" judgments, which need an argument. *(inferred)* This maps onto safety checks that block rather than steer.
- **Source.** SOC-S01, the passage on the sign.

## 8. The misology guard
- **What.** When an argument you trusted fails, blame your skill, not reason itself. Unskilled trust leads to cynicism.
- **Apply.** After a refutation or a failed product, ask whether the method was wrong or the hope was naive. Repair the method rather than declaring all evaluation worthless. Remember that "few are the good and few the evil": most cases sit in the middle.
- **Source.** SOC-S02, the misology interlude.

## 9. Causes versus conditions
- **What.** Mechanisms are the conditions without which something could not happen. The cause is the reason or end it serves.
- **Apply.** When an explanation lists only mechanisms, such as "because of the algorithm" or "because of the market", ask what was chosen, by whom and for what. *(inferred)* This is useful against "the model just did it".
- **Source.** SOC-S02, the autobiography (Anaxagoras).

## 10. The hypothesis raft (second-best method)
- **What.** Without certainty, adopt the strongest principle you have, accept what agrees with it, test its consequences, and sail on it while admitting the risk.
- **Apply.** Name the working hypothesis behind a proposal and what it would predict. Test one consequence. Keep the hypothesis only while it survives.
- **Source.** SOC-S02, the passages on the raft and the second-best method.
