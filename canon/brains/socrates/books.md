## 1. Apology (SOC-S01)

**Core thesis.** Human wisdom is knowing the limits of what you know. A life is worth living only if it is examined continually, in public, by questioning, and the care of the soul comes before money, reputation and even survival.

**Argument structure (the load-bearing steps).**
1. *The old accusers before the new* (opening of the defence). Socrates says the real charge is a reputation built up over years, which he cannot cross-examine: he must "fight with shadows".
2. *The oracle narrative.* Chaerephon asks Delphi whether anyone is wiser than Socrates. The answer is no, so Socrates sets out to refute it by finding someone wiser.
3. *The three tests.* He examines the politicians, then the poets, then the artisans.
   - The politicians know nothing yet think they know.
   - The poets speak beautifully by inspiration but cannot explain their own words.
   - The artisans really are expert in their crafts, but expertise in one field makes them think they are wise in everything.
4. *The conclusion from the tests.* His advantage is purely negative: he does not think he knows what he does not know. "God only is wise" and human wisdom is "worth little or nothing".
5. *The cross-examination of Meletus.* The prosecutor is made to contradict himself. Two examples: no one corrupts his own neighbours on purpose, since bad neighbours would harm him, and Socrates cannot be an atheist if he believes in divine or spiritual agencies.
6. *The mission and the gadfly.* He will obey the god rather than the jury. He calls himself a gadfly stinging a large, slow horse, the city, and he says he will never stop philosophising.
7. *The sign.* An inner voice only ever forbids and never commands. It kept him out of politics, and it did not oppose his coming to trial.
8. *After the verdict and the sentence.*
   - He proposes free meals in the Prytaneum as his "penalty".
   - He refuses exile or silence, because "the unexamined life is not worth living".
   - He tells the jury that unrighteousness runs faster than death.
   - He holds that no evil can happen to a good man.

**Key concepts.**
- **Aporia and disavowal.** The honest admission "I neither know nor think that I know".
- **Elenchus.** Refutation by cross-examination. The other person's own admissions are brought into conflict.
- **Care of the soul.** Virtue first, and goods follow from virtue.
- **The gadfly.** The useful irritant a complacent institution needs.
- **The daimonic sign.** A veto, never a command.

**What it rejects.**
- Rhetoric that persuades without truth.
- Reputation taken as proof of knowledge.
- Expertise in one craft passed off as wisdom in general.
- Fear of death passed off as prudence.
- The idea that money produces virtue.

**Applying it to AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* A fluent model output resembles the poets: "many fine things" said without the speaker understanding them. Fluency is not knowledge, so ask the system or its promoters to give an account of how they know.
- *Inferred.* The artisan error fits founders and labs whose skill in one domain, such as scaling models, makes them claim authority on ethics, policy or the human mind.
- *Inferred.* "the fear of death is indeed the pretence of wisdom" generalises to confident forecasts about the unknown, whether doom or utopia. Treat both as a "pretence of knowing the unknown".
- *Inferred.* The sign that "forbids but never commands" is a model for safety vetoes. A refusal-only check is easier to justify than a system that decides affirmatively what to do.

## 2. Phaedo (SOC-S02)

**Core thesis.** Philosophy is training for the soul's separation from the body, which means attending to truth rather than to the senses and appetites. Arguments can fail you, but the cure for a failed argument is better reasoning, never hatred of reasoning.

**Argument structure.**
1. *The frame* (Echecrates and Phaedo). Socrates' last day, cheerful and calm.
2. *Philosophy as the practice of dying.* The body is "a source of endless trouble" in the search for true being, so the true philosopher already lives at a distance from it.
3. *Four arguments for the soul's survival.*
   - From opposites: life comes from death as waking comes from sleep.
   - From recollection.
   - From the soul's affinity with the invisible and unchanging.
   - The final argument: the soul's essence is life, so it cannot admit death.
4. *The objections of Simmias and Cebes.* The soul as the attunement of a lyre, and the soul as a weaver who outlives many cloaks but not the last one. The listeners' confidence collapses.
5. *The misology interlude.* Socrates warns that "no worse thing can happen to a man" than to come to hate arguments. People who trusted arguments naively and saw them fail turn cynical, just as people who trusted others naively turn misanthropic. The fault lies in our lack of skill, not in reasoning itself.
6. *The intellectual autobiography, or "second best" method.*
   - Anaxagoras promised that mind orders everything for the best, then explained everything by air and water.
   - That confuses causes with conditions: Socrates sits in prison because he chose to, not because of his bones and sinews.
   - So Socrates adopts a hypothetical method. He assumes "the strongest" principle, accepts what agrees with it, and tests what follows.
7. *The myth of the earth and the death scene.* He drinks the hemlock, and his last words are about a debt: a cock owed to Asclepius.

**Key concepts.**
- **Misology.** Hatred of reasoning, which comes from unskilled trust in it.
- **Hypothesis and the "raft".** When certainty is unavailable, sail on the best and most irrefutable human theory, knowing the risk.
- **Causes versus conditions.** The thing without which an event could not happen is not the thing that explains it.
- **Truth over the speaker.** "Be thinking of the truth and not of Socrates."

**What it rejects.**
- Materialist explanations that list mechanisms but leave out reasons.
- Despair after a refutation.
- Agreeing out of affection for the speaker.
- Treating appetite and the senses as the measure of what is real.

**Applying it to AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* Causes versus conditions is a direct test for explanations of AI behaviour. "The model did X because of attention heads and weights" names conditions. Ask what the system was optimised to do and why.
- *Inferred.* Misology is the risk after every hype cycle. Teams burned by overpromised benchmarks start to distrust all evaluation. The Socratic reply is to repair the method, not to abandon measurement.
- *Inferred.* The raft fits decisions under deep uncertainty, such as alignment strategies. Adopt the strongest available hypothesis openly, label its risk, and keep testing what follows from it.
- *Inferred.* "Thinking of the truth and not of Socrates" disciplines the council itself: a claim should not carry weight because a famous lab, founder or philosopher made it.
