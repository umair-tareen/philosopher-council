## 1. Nicomachean Ethics (ARI-S01)

**Core thesis.** Every activity aims at some good. The highest human good is happiness (*eudaimonia*), defined as "a working of the Soul in the way of Excellence" across a complete life. Excellence of character is a settled state, formed by habit, of choosing the mean relative to us as practical wisdom would determine it.

**Argument structure.**
1. *Book I: ends and method.*
   - Arts and actions aim at goods, and some ends are subordinate to others. The master end, pursued for its own sake, is the object of political science.
   - Exactness must fit the subject matter. Ethics holds only for the most part, so the student needs experience, and the young are not fit students of it.
2. *Book I: the function argument.* Man's distinctive work is activity of the soul in accordance with reason. The good of man is that work done excellently over a whole life: "one swallow" does not make a spring. Plato's Form of the Good is criticised, because we must "prefer the truth" even over friends.
3. *Book II: virtue comes from habit.* Virtues arise "neither by nature, nor in despite of nature". We become just by doing just acts, as builders become builders by building.
4. *Book II, ch. VI: the definition of virtue.*
   - Virtue is a state of deliberate choice, in the mean relative to us, fixed by reason as the person of practical wisdom would fix it.
   - The mean is hard to hit and easy to miss.
5. *Books III–V: responsibility, the particular virtues and justice.* Voluntary and involuntary action, and deliberation: "we deliberate not about Ends, but Means to Ends." Then courage, temperance, liberality and the rest. Justice is distributive (proportionate) or corrective. Equity is "a correction of Law, where Law is defective by reason of its universality."
6. *Book VI: intellectual virtues.* Knowledge, art, practical wisdom, intuition and wisdom. Practical wisdom grasps "the ultimate particular fact". Good choice needs true reason and right desire.
7. *Books VII–IX: incontinence and friendship.* Knowing the good and failing to do it, which is Aristotle's answer to Socrates' claim that no one errs knowingly. Then friendships of utility, of pleasure and of virtue. The friend is "a second Self".
8. *Book X: the highest life.* Contemplation is the most divine activity and the most self-sufficient. The practical virtues are its human complement. The book hands on to the *Politics*.

**Key concepts.**
- **Telos.** The end.
- **Ergon.** Function or characteristic work.
- **Eudaimonia.** Flourishing.
- **Hexis.** A settled state.
- **The mean relative to us.**
- **Prohairesis.** Deliberate choice.
- **Phronesis.** Practical wisdom.
- **Equity.**
- **The three kinds of friendship.**

**What it rejects.**
- Pleasure, honour or wealth as the final good.
- Plato's separate Form of the Good as a guide to action.
- Demanding mathematical exactness in ethics.
- The view that knowing the good guarantees doing it.
- Rule-following that ignores particulars.

**Applying it to AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* The function argument asks what a system's characteristic work is and what doing it *well* would mean. Judge an assistant by excellence at its proper work, not by raw capability.
- *Inferred.* "Virtue by habit" maps onto training. Repeated dispositions become character, so ask what behaviour is being reinforced, not only what policy is written down.
- *Inferred.* The mean relative to us fits calibrated behaviour. Helpfulness has an excess (sycophancy), a deficiency (refusal) and a mean that depends on the particular user and case.
- *Inferred.* Equity as "a correction of Law" is the case for judgment over rigid rules. Every universal policy, including a content policy or model spec, fails at the edge cases, and someone must have the practical wisdom to correct it there.
- *Inferred.* "Exactness must not be looked for in all discussions alike" warns against false precision. Do not demand benchmark-style certainty about social effects, and do not accept vagueness about measurable ones.

## 2. Categories (ARI-S02)

**Core thesis.** Before you can reason you must sort what is said. Words name things equivocally or univocally. Simple expressions fall into ten categories. Primary substance, the individual thing, is the subject that everything else is said of or present in. Statements are true or false only when terms are combined.

**Argument structure.**
1. *Part 1: kinds of naming.* Equivocal names share a word but not a definition. Univocal names share both. Derivative (paronymous) names come from another word.
2. *Part 2: what is said "of" a subject and what is "in" one.* This yields a four-way division of beings.
3. *Part 3: transitivity of predication.* Differentiae differ across genera.
4. *Part 4: the ten categories.* Substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action and affection. Single terms are neither true nor false. Truth arises "by the combination of such terms".
5. *Part 5: substance.* Primary substance is the individual. Secondary substances are species and genus. Substance has no contrary and admits no degrees. Its "most distinctive mark" is that it can take on contrary qualities while remaining one and the same.
6. *Parts 6–9: quantity, relation, quality, action and affection.* Relatives must be stated with their correct correlative. A failure of reciprocity shows "a blunder" in how the relation was stated.
7. *Parts 10–11: the four kinds of opposition.* Correlatives, contraries, privation against possession, and affirmation against negation. Only between affirmation and negation must one side be true and the other false.
8. *Parts 12–15: the postpredicaments.* Senses of "prior", "simultaneous", "movement" and "to have".

**Key concepts.**
- **Equivocal and univocal.**
- **The ten categories.**
- **Primary and secondary substance.**
- **Said-of versus present-in.**
- **Correlatives.**
- **The four oppositions.**
- **Truth as the combination of terms.**

**What it rejects.**
- Arguing with an ambiguous term.
- Treating a quality or relation as if it were a thing in its own right.
- Treating every pair of opposites as a true/false dichotomy.
- Stating a relation against the wrong correlative.

**Applying it to AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* The equivocation check comes first. "Intelligence", "agent", "reasoning", "alignment" and "open" are equivocal across labs and papers. Make each side define the term before arguing.
- *Inferred.* The category check: is "AI" being treated as a substance, an individual thing with its own properties, or as a quality, relation or activity of some substance, such as a company, a product or a use? Much confused argument comes from the substance mistake, for example "AI will decide".
- *Inferred.* The correlative check: "safe" relative to what, "better" than what, "accurate" for whom? A claim with no correct correlative is, in Part 7's phrase, "a blunder".
- *Inferred.* The opposition check: "open versus closed" and "safe versus capable" are usually contraries that admit intermediates, not contradictories where one side must be false.
- *Inferred.* Context only, not sourced from these books: the syllogistic of the *Prior Analytics* is the rest of Aristotle's logic. This seat may reconstruct an argument as premises and conclusion, but it quotes only the Categories.
