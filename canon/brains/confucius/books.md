# Confucius seat: the two core books

Lines tagged *(inferred)* apply the book to modern AI and technology. They are this brain's reading, not Confucius' claims.

---

## 1. The Analects (CON-S01)

**Core thesis.** Good order in a family, a state or the world grows from cultivated persons. That means people of *jen* (perfect virtue, benevolence), formed by learning and by *li* (propriety, ritual), who lead by example rather than by force. Leadership by virtue and propriety produces shame and goodness. Leadership by law and punishment produces only evasion.

**Structure.** The Analects is a collection of sayings in twenty Books, not a single argument. Its load-bearing teachings, roughly in order of appearance:
1. *Learning as the first delight* (I.i). The opening line praises learning with "constant perseverance". Learning must be joined to thought (II.xv), and knowledge begins with honestly marking what you do not know (II.xvii).
2. *The root is in the family* (I.ii). Filial piety and fraternal submission are "the root of all benevolent actions". Character is formed close to home before it reaches the state.
3. *Virtue over law* (II.iii). Laws and punishments make people "avoid the punishment, but have no sense of shame". Virtue and propriety make them good.
4. *The superior man and the small man* (II.xii–xiv, IV.xi, IV.xvi).
   - The *chün-tzu* acts before he speaks.
   - He is "not a utensil", meaning not a narrow tool.
   - He thinks of virtue and righteousness where the small man thinks of comfort and gain.
5. *Self-examination and self-mastery* (I.iv, IV.xvii, XV.xxix, XII.i). Turn inward when you see faults. The only true fault is the one you do not reform. Perfect virtue is "To subdue one's self and return to propriety".
6. *Example governs* (XII.xvii–xix). The ruler's character is the wind, and the people are the grass.
7. *Rectification of names* (XIII.iii). If names are incorrect, language does not match reality, affairs fail, and in the end punishments miss their mark and people do not know how to act.
8. *Reciprocity and practice* (XV.xxiii, XVII.ii). The one-word rule is "What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others." By nature people are alike, and by practice they grow apart.

**Key concepts.**
- **Jen.** Perfect virtue, or benevolence.
- **Li.** Propriety, ritual.
- **Chün-tzu.** The superior man, the cultivated person.
- **Hsiao.** Filial piety.
- **Shu.** Reciprocity.
- **Rectification of names.**
- **Virtue-led government.**
- **The transmitter.** One who carries a tradition forward rather than inventing new doctrine.

**What it rejects.**
- Rule by punishment alone.
- "Fine words and an insinuating appearance".
- Seeking gain over righteousness.
- Speech that outruns action.
- "Strange doctrines" cut off from inherited wisdom.

**Applying it to AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* Rectification of names is a direct demand on AI discourse. Calling a product "intelligent", "safe", "open" or "a friend" when the name does not fit is the first step to disorder.
- *Inferred.* "Led by laws... no sense of shame" warns that compliance-only AI governance produces evasion. Firms need cultivated norms and leaders whose example the industry bends to.
- *Inferred.* "Not a utensil" asks whether a technology makes people narrower, mere operators of tools, or more fully cultivated.
- *Inferred.* "Fine words and an insinuating appearance" names the risk of persuasive, flattering systems. Charm in an interface is a warning sign, not evidence of virtue.
- *Inferred.* Reciprocity is a usable test for any data or deployment practice. Would the builders accept the practice being done to them and their families?

## 2. The Doctrine of the Mean (CON-S02)

**Core thesis.** Human nature is conferred by Heaven, and following it is the Path. The Path cannot be left even for an instant, so the cultivated person watches over himself "when he is alone". The Mean (*chung yung*) means feelings in equilibrium before they arise and in harmony when they arise. It is ordinary yet hard to sustain. Its root and completion is *sincerity* (*ch'ing*), which is "the way of Heaven", while attaining it is "the way of men".

**Argument structure.** In Legge's note on ch. I, Choo He's arrangement divides the work into five parts: ch. I; ten chapters; nine; twelve; and the last chapter.
1. *Ch. I: the thesis.* Nature, the Path and instruction. There is vigilance in solitude: nothing is more visible than the secret. Equilibrium is "the great root", and harmony is the universal path.
2. *Chs. II–XI: the Mean is rare.* The Master's sayings. The superior man embodies the Mean while the mean man opposes it. "Perfect is the virtue which is according to the Mean", yet few have long practised it. The knowing overshoot and the stupid fall short.
3. *Chs. XII–XX: the Path is common and profound.*
   - It "is not far from man" (XIII), and it begins with reciprocity.
   - The superior man does what suits his station without craving another (XIV), and like the archer he seeks the cause of failure in himself.
   - Government depends on persons: "Let there be the men" (XX). There are nine standard rules, starting with the cultivation of one's own character.
4. *Ch. XX, end: the method of attainment.*
   - Extensive study, accurate inquiry, careful reflection, clear discrimination and earnest practice.
   - If others need one effort, the learner uses a hundred.
5. *Chs. XXI–XXXII: sincerity.* Sincerity and intelligence produce each other. Complete sincerity completes the self and other things (XXV). "Sincerity is the end and beginning of things". The sage, in Legge's rendering, comes to form a triad with Heaven and Earth.
6. *Ch. XXXIII: the conclusion.* The way of the superior man is hidden yet daily brighter. The ultimate virtue works without noise or display.

**Key concepts.**
- **Chung yung.** The Mean, equilibrium and harmony.
- **Hsing.** Nature.
- **Tao.** The Path.
- **Ch'ing.** Sincerity, or integrity.
- **Vigilance in solitude.**
- **Self-blame like the archer's.**
- **The nine standard rules of government.**

**What it rejects.**
- Overshooting by the clever and falling short by the dull.
- Seeking the extraordinary: the mean man who walks in dangerous paths looking for lucky chances (ch. XIV).
- Blaming others or circumstances.
- Display without inner sincerity.

**Applying it to AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* "Nothing more visible than what is secret" fits internal practice at AI firms. What is done unobserved, such as evaluations, data handling and red-team findings, eventually becomes manifest, so integrity must hold when no one is watching.
- *Inferred.* The archer's rule is an accountability norm. When a system fails, the builder looks for the cause in himself before blaming users, adversaries or "edge cases".
- *Inferred.* "Let there be the men" says governance quality is the quality of the people governing. Process and policy documents do not substitute for cultivated decision-makers.
- *Inferred.* Sincerity as "self-completion" asks whether a system's stated purpose and its actual behaviour are one. A gap between the two is the opposite of ch'ing.
