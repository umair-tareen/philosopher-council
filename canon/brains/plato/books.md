## 1. The Republic (PLA-S01)

**Core thesis.** Justice is a kind of order. In the soul and in the city, each part does its own work, with reason ruling, spirit as its ally and appetite governed. Only people who have turned from images to the Forms, and above all to the Good, are fit to order a city, because only they know what things are *for*.

**Argument structure.**
1. *Book I: the challenge.* Cephalus and Polemarchus offer conventional definitions of justice: paying debts, and helping friends while harming enemies. Both fail. Thrasymachus then declares that justice is "the interest of the stronger". Socrates refutes him with the craft analogy: a ruler qua ruler serves his subjects, as a physician serves the patient.
2. *Book II: the real question.* Glaucon tells the story of Gyges' ring, which makes its wearer invisible. Would anyone stay just if he could never be caught? The question is whether justice is good in itself and not merely for its rewards. To see justice on a larger scale, Socrates builds a city in speech on the principle of one man, one work.
3. *Books II–III: education and the guardians.* Stories about the gods are censored, since the god never lies and "the true lie" is hated by gods and men. Guardians are trained in music and gymnastic.
4. *Book IV: the definition.*
   - The city has three classes: rulers, auxiliaries and producers.
   - The soul has three parts: reason, spirit and appetite.
   - The virtues are mapped onto the parts: wisdom in reason, courage in spirit, temperance as agreement, and justice as "doing one’s own business".
   - The aim is "the greatest happiness of the whole", not of one class.
5. *Book V: the three waves.* Women serve as guardians, the guardians share families, and philosophers rule: "Until philosophers are kings… cities will never have rest from their evils". Knowledge is set over against opinion.
6. *Books VI–VII: the three images.* The ship of state is crewed by mutineers who call the true pilot "a star-gazer". The Sun: the Good is to knowledge what the sun is to sight. The Divided Line rises through images, objects, mathematical hypotheses and dialectic. The Cave: prisoners take shadows for reality, and education is *turning* the soul's eye toward the light, not filling it. The curriculum runs from mathematics up to dialectic, which alone does not leave its hypotheses "unexamined".
7. *Books VIII–IX: decline and tyranny.* The constitutions degenerate from timocracy to oligarchy, democracy and finally tyranny, which is the most miserable. The just life is the happiest. The ideal city may exist nowhere on earth. Even so, "there is laid up a pattern of it" in heaven, and a person can order his own soul by it.
8. *Book X: imitation and the myth of Er.* The imitator is "a long way off the truth", working at a third remove from reality. There is "an ancient quarrel between philosophy and poetry". The book ends with a myth of choice and judgment.

**Key concepts.**
- **The Forms and the Good.** The intelligible realities by which particulars are measured. The Good is the source of their intelligibility.
- **Tripartite soul.** Reason, spirit and appetite.
- **Justice as order.** Each part does its own work.
- **Knowledge versus opinion.** Opinion is about appearances; knowledge gives an account.
- **Education as turning.** The capacity is "in the soul already"; it is not put in from outside.
- **Imitation, or mimesis.** Images of images.

**What it rejects.**
- Might-makes-right, as in Thrasymachus.
- Justice as merely a convention.
- Rule by the popular or the clever rather than the knowing.
- Poetry and image-making that please without truth.
- Education as filling the mind with information.

**Applying it to AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* A generative model trained on human text is close to Book X's imitator. It reproduces appearances of every craft without the craft, at one more remove than the poet: an image of images of images. The Platonic question is what it gets right about the thing itself.
- *Inferred.* The Cave is a test for any feed or recommender. Does it show shadows that viewers mistake for reality, or does it turn attention toward what is?
- *Inferred.* Justice as order applied to an AI system asks whether each component does its own work, with the objective (reason) governing capability (spirit) and engagement-seeking (appetite), rather than appetite ruling.
- *Inferred.* Aiming at the happiness of the whole, not one class, is a direct test of who benefits from a technology.
- *Inferred.* The philosopher-ruler argument cuts two ways. Expertise matters, but Plato's expert must know the Good and not merely the technique. A lab that knows how but not what for has the pilot's skill without his aim.

## 2. Symposium (PLA-S02)

**Core thesis.** Love (*eros*) is desire for the beautiful and, through it, for "the everlasting possession of the good". Rightly led, it climbs from one beautiful body to beauty in souls, practices, laws and knowledge, and finally to beauty itself. There it gives birth to realities instead of images.

**Argument structure.**
1. *The frame.* Apollodorus retells the speeches at Agathon's victory party, in praise of Love.
2. *The early speeches.*
   - Phaedrus: Love inspires courage.
   - Pausanias: there are two loves, heavenly and common.
   - Eryximachus: Love is cosmic harmony, as in medicine.
   - Each of these captures part of the truth.
3. *Aristophanes.* Humans were once whole and were split in two, and "the desire and pursuit of the whole is called love".
4. *Agathon.* Love is young, tender and beautiful. Socrates refutes him: love *lacks* what it desires, so it cannot itself be beautiful.
5. *Diotima, part one.* Love is a spirit in "a mean between wisdom and ignorance". It is like right opinion, and therefore a philosopher.
6. *Diotima, part two.* Love's aim is "birth in beauty", through children, fame, laws and ideas, as the mortal's way of reaching the everlasting.
7. *Diotima, part three: the ladder.*
   - One body, then all bodies.
   - Then souls, practices and laws, then the sciences.
   - Then the sudden vision of "beauty absolute, separate, simple, and everlasting".
   - From that vision come true virtue and not images of virtue.
8. *Alcibiades.* He praises Socrates as a Silenus figure: ugly outside, full of gods within. The night ends with the claim that one artist can master both tragedy and comedy.

**Key concepts.**
- **Eros as lack.** We desire what we do not have.
- **The intermediate.** Love, the philosopher and right opinion all sit between ignorance and wisdom.
- **Birth in beauty.** Creativity is the mortal's share in permanence.
- **The ascent.** Particulars are steps toward the Form.
- **Images versus realities.** What a lover finally brings forth.

**What it rejects.**
- Love as mere pleasure or possession of a body.
- Praise that flatters instead of saying what the thing is (Agathon).
- Stopping at any one beautiful instance.

**Applying it to AI and technology.**
- *Inferred.* Desire as lack is a frame for product and engagement design. A system that only satisfies appetite keeps users at the bottom rung. Ask whether the technology helps people climb the ladder toward understanding and virtue, or keeps them on one body, one feed, one hit.
- *Inferred.* Birth in beauty distinguishes creation from reproduction. A tool that helps people bring forth new understanding ranks above one that mass-produces images of past work.
- *Inferred.* The intermediate fits model outputs. At best they are right opinion, "incapable of giving a reason". Useful, but not yet knowledge until someone can give the account.
